# Maximizing Social Energy as an Introvert

Years ago, two of my friends and I were lounging around after the conclusion of our book club. It was getting late, and after continuously inching lower in my chair, I told my friends that I was a bit tired and will head out soon.

One of my friends, Patrick, remarked: “Yeah, I can tell. Whenever you’re about to crash, you get super quiet.”

His offhand comment hit me like a thunderclap. For most of my life, I perceived social battery drainage as something that happened *to* me, not as something I could control. It took that moment to realize that my social battery wasn’t some mysterious force – I could understand, work around, and work *with* my introversion.

I started writing this post in 2020. Since then, I’ve continually thought about and refined my approach to social energy. If this resonates with you, I hope it can be helpful. My qualifications are that I’ve spent two plus decades of my life as a rather unhealthy introvert, and in the last few years as a better one.

---

## Social battery: Recognizing drainage and drainage cues

For any given event, my social battery chart looks something like this:

![An introvert's social energy chart over time](/assets/social-energy-chart.jpeg){width=300}

I think everyone’s social battery chart is distinct. Distinct, but not unique – so it’s possible to find similar models across friends and mentors and adopt working strategies. For me, my social battery chart features two patterns:

1. Dramatically different drainage depending on talking vs. doing activities, and

2. A significant dropoff at the tail end (the “cliff”)

The cliff was what Patrick noticed at the end of our book club. I started paying attention to my battery levels after he pointed it out, and found that I always had a “cliff” no matter the social event type or length of time, and that it was always better to politely recuse myself from the social situation *before* I fall off the edge of the cliff than after.

Some of my introverted friends don’t seem to have social energy cliffs. Instead, they taper gracefully down to a resting position on a couch, on which they’re content to stay until the end of the party.

I also found that the number one predictor of how fast my social battery would drain, even more than how many people I knew at the event, was whether the event was activity-based or talking-based. I lasted significantly longer, and sometimes even felt *energized* when participating in an activity-based event - even when it’s inherently extroverted.

![The introvert multiverse](/assets/introvert-multiverse.jpeg){width=300}

**Being in the right social environment matters because it maximizes your time, energy, and sociability.** All else being equal, your social energy will burn out slower in the right environment (more bang for your buck).

I think it’s worthwhile for every introvert to sit down and identify their social battery’s characteristics.

---

## Working around your particular battery chart

Knowing how your battery drains is the first step to understanding how to maximize its lifespan. For my case, this manifested in two learnings. I wanted to:

1. Manipulate my social calendar to my advantage, and

2. Have pre-planned escape hatches.

Like most introverts, I feel a level of obligation towards certain events and people. What helped most for me was: 1. building the strength to decline without FOMO (still a struggle for me), and 2. rebuilding social events to be advantageous to my battery. Example variations I sometimes make to orient catchups towards activity:

- **Works alright:** Grabbing dinner at a restaurant → **Works better:** Making dinner together

- **Works alright:** Catching up at home → **Works better:** Board games at home

- **Works alright:** Happy hour at the end of work summit → **Works better:** Escape room at the end of work summit

I find that in many cases, I can keep both talking and activity aspects if I *sequence* it correctly. Going from activity → talking (ex. dance class → dinner after) promotes much greater connection than the other way around.

I also think having an “escape hatch” is important. Sometimes there’s simply a natural end to an activity; other times, socialization closes need to be preplanned. I’m not saying to lie — it’s best if the escape hatches are honest explanations of why one needs to leave. If all else fails, it pays to learn how to be honest and say “hey, I’m social’ed out, but it was great catching up.”

![Escape hatches from social events](/assets/escape-hatches.jpeg){width=400}

Strategies might have to change between locations and groups of people. For example, the strategies I used in San Francisco weren’t efficient for New York, where it was easy for hangouts to bleed into each other without a natural end.

Also, despite your best efforts, you probably won’t get to full battery optimization. You’ll need to make sacrifices – for work, for other responsibilities, for a loved one – where extroversion is required above your desired line. A healthy balance of sacrifice is a good thing, and that took me a long time to learn.

## Adapting for yourself

My social battery is probably different from your social battery. There’s many axes of consideration for social environments, but there are bound to be one or two that are most impactful for you. A few to get you thinking:

- Groups or one-on-ones?

- Talking-based activities or action-based activities?

- Familiar or foreign environments?

- In person or over a social media/web-based forum?

- Hosting or attending?

- What about the ratio of people you know to people you don’t know?

As a result, your methods of adapting to your social battery are probably different from mine. Maybe you optimize for hosting dinner parties, realizing that hosting affords you the greatest control over who you interact with and what the group does. Maybe you try to find a small group to wind down with every event, knowing that tapering down together will help you with the natural end to your energy.

Along the way, maybe you’ll think as I have: “This is *so much additional work.* Why was I born to have to think about this?”

I have thought a lot about this over the years and have so much to share (maybe in part two). But I also think there’s wisdom in not over-analyzing why something is or is not and simply solving for the realities of life. I like this quote by Isabelle Myers-Briggs: “The best-adjusted people are the psychologically patriotic, who are glad to be what they are.”

I’m always curious how different introverts have developed their repertoire of social strategies. I hope this was interesting, and share with me what’s worked for you.
