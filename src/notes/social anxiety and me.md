# Social Anxiety and Me

Sometime around 2015, I started to dread social events.

In a healthy interaction, my attention is outward: I’m listening to the people I’m interacting with, I’m considering their point of view, their emotions and their needs, and I’m responding in kind without a lack of confidence in who I am. But when I developed anxiety, my world fell inwards: I spent interactions in internal loops of negativity, sure that I have nothing to say, that everyone was wondering why I was so quiet, and that they’re talking about me behind my back. Physically, I had sweaty palms, a racing heart, and short breaths. Events often culminated in the intense need to get out of the situation and be alone. The malicious part is that the loop feeds on itself - I’d feel anxious, which resulted in anxious behaviour, which made me feel anxious, which resulted in anxious behaviour… on and on.

I had difficulty putting a name to how I was feeling. It wasn’t introversion. Introversion and social anxiety aren’t interchangeable; they aren’t even synonyms. I’ve been an introvert my whole life, and although I need a lot of alone time, introversion doesn’t prevent me from being authentic during the time I spend with others. It wasn’t shyness either. I was a bit of an awkward kid (we all were), but this didn’t match the general self-consciousness I felt when I was fifteen. It was only later that I put my feelings and behaviour to a term that made sense to me: *social anxiety*.

I can pinpoint the start of my social anxiety. David had graduated and moved to Seattle for work; I was still a college junior at UCLA. We had started dating only half a year ago, and were now trying to lead very separate lives in long distance. David made a lovely new group of friends, and for whatever reason, I clammed up when I met them. We argued about my behaviour. David was upset that I didn’t put in effort to get to know his friends, and I was upset that he didn’t understand why I found it difficult to penetrate their tight circle. The more we argued, the worse my anxiety got. It blossomed into a shadow that followed me for the next six years. His presence became a trigger for these feelings, regardless of what event or who we were with.

It bled into different friend groups. It bled into my evaluation of my self-worth. It made me acutely aware of the difference between me with anxiety and me without anxiety. The contrast in my “selves” became a source of anxiety on its own. I pulled back from getting to know others because I was too in my head to truly listen, so I relegated myself to the sidelines of the team effort that was forming friendships. My social anxiety was most prominent in situations with David, so I tried and failed to reconcile that someone I loved very much was also the source of much pain.

David and I, burned out and tired from our arguments, came to the quiet agreement that we could use our energy towards fixing the problem instead. “How can I help?” David asked. The question was a simple acknowledgement: “hey, you’re going through something tough. Let’s tackle it together.” That was perhaps the most important step forward we took, and it reminds me of a section I highlighted from the book *I Hear You* earlier this year:

> “It’s very difficult for someone to work through difficult issues when they’re blinded by strong emotions. Painful or difficult emotions get stronger and more intimidating when fought or suppressed. When you validate other people, you help them see and accept their emotions for what they are: just feelings—neither good nor bad. This makes it significantly easier for them to process them and break free.”

Following that, we came up with a system where he would always stick by my side when we went to an event. He would guide me into groups and conversations so I could participate. As it progresses, we may separate into different groups. At touchpoints throughout the event, David would come back and ask how I was doing. If at any point I said I needed him by my side, he would return without hesitation.

I remember it feeling rather forced at the beginning. In fact, it added another anxiety to the list: the worry that I was preventing David from living His Best Life<sup>TM</sup> because he needed to be by my nervous, unfun side. But I’m glad we persisted, because it actually worked. We executed this strategy for a while. Then at some point… I started needing David less. “Go have fun!” I would say, nervous but pushing myself to progress. I still appreciated the check-ins though, until I started waving those off too. I started being able to speak up with people that I knew well - and then with people who I didn’t know at all. I no longer felt lesser-than. At its worst, my mind was consumed by judging itself. Slowly, I was able to wrestle some of that energy back so I could listen to others. It wasn’t linear, obviously, and it wasn’t fast, but looking back I can definitely tell that it was improving over the years. At a recent event, I faced all the conditions that would have crippled me five years ago: it was large, it was long, there were many people I knew only peripherally or didn’t know at all, and David had to be elsewhere for a good portion of it. After the event was all over, I sat in our apartment, blinked, and was hit by a truck: it was gone.

For me, social anxiety faded in the same way that relationships do. I’ve always thought relationships died twice: once when it ends, and one final time when you realize they no longer cross your mind. My social anxiety died long before I realized I was no longer affected by it. Even now, I’m starting to forget how debilitating it was those years back. It’s only when I riffle through my journals do I remember: when I was going through it, I thought it’d never end.

Why don’t we talk about social anxiety more often? It’s one of the most common anxiety disorders, so I’d bet good money that someone I know – maybe even someone I know quite well – is going through it. Then I realized that oh yeah, I didn’t talk about this with anyone besides David, and even then I only talked about it as it related to us. So one of the purposes behind this blog post is to share my story of social anxiety in the hopes that my specific circumstances and specific solutions can be of use to a friend who reads this.

The second reason is more selfish: writing all of this down is cathartic. I have the benefit of hindsight and the optimism of having made it through the tunnel, so right now is the best time to wrap up a chapter now that I know how it starts and ends. Time tends to dull old hurts and old successes equally, and for me this is a story of both that I don’t want to forget. In case social anxiety rears its ugly head again (knock on wood), I’ll have this post to refer back to. In the meantime, I just want to enjoy the end of the journey. Six years is quite long when you’re twenty-seven. I feel like I can breathe easier now. It’s nice.
