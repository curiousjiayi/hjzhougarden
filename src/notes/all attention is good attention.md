# All attention is good attention

A few months ago I started a social media account in my niche; although I now have a 5-digit following count, I’m very much still a little mouse on the social media Serengeti, and so I get inundated by reels and carousels and captions and comments on growth tips; even when I don’t actively search for this information it’s spoon fed to me, because of course who wouldn’t like to grow a little bigger? Who wouldn’t want to be a lion instead of a field mouse?

Here is a selection of advice that has been given to me (I use “given” liberally, since the advice wasn’t offered *to* me specifically; what I mean is that I was scrolling and saw these posts)–some useful and some horrendous, in no particular order:

Study your viewers and post when they’re most active; the algorithm doesn’t care when you post so just post three times a day, five, ten, as many times as you can; use creator search insights; use the latest feature because the platform is promoting it; I got fired from Tiktok after 3 years so here’s all the tea! (no, they never worked at Tiktok); interact with others after posting; the algorithm doesn’t care if you interact; hook your viewers in the first 3 seconds; use emotional triggers; make them feel like “omg, that’s me”; get your viewers arguing in the comments; search for trends; delete flopped videos; never delete flopped videos; create within your niche, otherwise the platform won’t know how to categorize you; screw your niche; and don’t forget–like, comment, and send this video to a friend so we can all grow together.

The market economy of attention is fierce and competitive in ways that mirror the economy of labour and money, so the obvious question is: isn’t attention just a proxy for cold, hard cash? But I think attention lives as a separate layer, traded across even the wealthy as a resource of its own. Think of Becca Bloom; if you’re unfamiliar with the name, Becca Bloom is an American (Silicon Valley) #richtok influencer known for her “relatable rich” persona. It’s unclear what her family’s net worth is, but the common refrain is that she’s a billionaire heiress.

Let’s say that I had the easiest job in the world: for every 1 second I spent counting the time, I would make $1. So in a minute, I’d make $60, I’d have an hourly wage of $3,600, and if I worked a solid 8-hour day, 5 days a week, for a month, my monthly salary would be a pretty half a million. This is a cushy job, so each year I take 4 weeks of vacation; that means, when working 48 weeks, I make just shy of $7M a year. But of course I’m not an idiot, so instead of doing extraneous things like sleeping, eating, and otherwise living, I dedicate every second of my life to counting seconds. This greatly maximizes my earnings and now I make $31M a year. At this incredible and inhuman pace, it would still take me *31 years* to reach 1 billion dollars. Now that’s a career!

Gossip on the block is that Becca Bloom isn’t worth quite as much as she portrays, but I’m neither caught up on these rumours nor do I want it to be the focus for this essay; regardless, I think it’s safe to say that she has an amount of wealth most of us will never amass. Thus my question is: *why* in the world is Becca Bloom wasting her time on social media? Your guess is as good as mine; I don’t know her and don’t want to make assumptions, but I think she’s emblematic of a wider point: even at exorbitant levels of wealth–a level which by all accounts should place one at the tippy top, poacher-in-the-Serengeti levels of animalistic hierarchy–people are interested in and willing to fight in the market economy of attention.

All attention is good attention; to question it is akin to questioning why people earn money. There are many unsavory ways to earn a living that are disparaged–blood money, speculation and gambling, shady industries–but we more frequently nod in understanding when our friends say: “well, it’s not my calling, but it pays the bills.” A career requires sacrifice and existential dread in our younger years; later on, when we’re established and knowledgeable, we look back on our nights of sitting in front of a computer pushing pixels because the squares in our presentation need to be aligned *just right* as worthy sacrifices in the climb. There’s a similar trajectory in attention as a profession; to reach the upper echelons, engagement farming and rage baiting is simply apprenticeship.

You don’t have to believe in capital systems to know that it moves the world, and similarly you don’t personally need to prioritize the attention economy to understand that attention is a very real, very impactful resource that can be bought, haggled, expended, stolen. The difference, maybe? is that attention is completely intangible, unbacked by any authority with an army and liable to completely vanish on a whim. What happens to the 2 *billion* dollar pre-product, pre-revenue, pre-direction seed round raised by Thinking Machines if skepticism continues to boil and talent leaks out? Or Cluely’s more humble $20M funding that seemingly goes exclusively towards making viral reels? No matter; attention first, value later. As gen Z grows in the workforce industries move with them, and this is a generation that lives, breathes, thinks in attention.

In Thompson’s wonderful [26 Most Important Ideas For 2026](https://www.derekthompson.org/p/the-26-most-important-ideas-for-2026), #10 notes–underneath a WSJ chart showing a nosedive in patriotism, religion, family, and community involvement–that “the only currency is currency… What binds the modern world is our collective reverence for money.” What’s missing, probably because all the researchers at WSJ and the University of Chicago are [uncs](https://www.google.com/search?q=what+is+an+unc), is attention. If attention was there on the chart, it would break the y-axis; a straight line going up, up, so high that it replaces the heavens people once looked to for meaning.
