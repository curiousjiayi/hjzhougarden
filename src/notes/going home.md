# Going Home

When I stepped off the plane to China, I felt at once that I had come home to a world that has moved on without me.

I was born in Shenzhen, in southern China. When we were young, my grandpa would take my cousin and I to pick bamboo shoots in the garden of my grandparent’s rusty old apartment complex. Life is always purest when you’re young; for me, like for many immigrants, it was the only time I had before my cultural identity split.

My family immigrated to Canada in 1999, when I was five years old. I don't remember the transition being hard. Like most kids my age did, I molded to my environment and learned the language, culture, and mannerisms around me. 8 years later, we reversed tracks and moved back – this time to Shanghai. I joined an international school and again melted into a beautifully melded world of an education curriculum in English, Chinese KTV on the weekends, and easy Chinglish with friends.

In 2012, I graduated high school and flew to America for college. That was the last time I left "for good." I revisited during summer holidays between school years, but that evaporated when I graduated college and started work. The last time I returned to China was in 2016; in the blink of an eye, 7 years had gone by. My memories of my home country were like bubbles floating on the surface of water: beautiful, rosy, delicate.

In the years that I resided in Shanghai, between 2007-2012, the country was in practical terms economically and politically capitalist - not so different from the west. The Great Firewall has always existed, but content moderation wasn't really there at a UGC level. Shanghai was a thriving port of expats of many different ethnic backgrounds, and it was possible for all of them to navigate Chinese society successfully. Over the years, this changed. When I stepped off the plane, I realized that China had built a huge plastic dome over its people. I stood enviously on the outside, watching the enclosed ecosystem like a child looking at fish.

## Inside the fishbowl

China has a penchant for "superapps", and WeChat and Alipay rule over all. In our two weeks in China, we did *everything* through scanning a QR code - from ordering food (servers don’t take orders anymore), to buying clothes and joining the brand membership, to paying for parking, to receiving our heavily Photoshopped hanfu photoshoot pictures. The entire system is as smooth as silk because everybody - from Brunello Cucinelli to the streetside hot and sour noodle vendor - is on it. When David and I paid in cash at a restaurant, a poor server had to get on his knees to fish a small ziploc bag of change out of the dusty back of a cabinet. After only two weeks, pulling out my wallet when I returned to the US felt antiquated.

But to use either WeChat or Alipay as a payment method, we needed to have a Chinese-issued bank card - an impossible requirement for tourists visiting for a short period of time. We were only able to get by because I borrowed my dad's secondary phone. Without that, David and I were thirty-year old adult-babies, unable to even pay for a cup of coffee.

So long as we had my dad’s charity though, we could explore all that Shenzhen and Chongqing had to offer. First, the perks of just simply being in China: food was mouth-watering. We ate Sichuanese hotpot in a bustling restaurant carved into the mountainside, Hunan griddle yellow beef, Shaanxi biangbiang noodles, Cantonese (Guangdong) dimsum, Hokkien chicken; the food on the bustling bottom floor of the mall was just as good as at an upscale restaurant.

Public transportation infrastructure was even more glorious as I remembered. Subways lace the city, last-mile taxi is easily hailable off the side of the street, and intercity high speed rails connect a large number of high tier cities. High-rises were required to allocate for green spaces, so glass buildings emerged from verdant tropical trees. The streets were pristine, and the highways were lined with flowers. This last part hasn't changed since I left; I just gained a greater appreciation for it after seven years of navigating around human feces in San Francisco.

Shenzhen is a relatively new city, so as futuristic as it is, it can come across as sterile. We got to see a more balanced blend of new and old China when we flew to Chongqing for three days to visit my father’s side. Chongqing [blew up on Tiktok](https://www.tiktok.com/@yasminvroon/video/7237178714616778010) as a cyberpunk mountain city where you can climb to the 27th floor and exit out to a ground floor, but it also is an old city with a historical root. The government’s managed to balance out the two well, encouraging new building development in the style of old Chinese architecture while electric taxis whiz around the city.

On that topic, an astonishing 25% of new cars sold each year in China were electric - one in four cars! - compared to 6~8% in the US. Sparkling car retailers in malls displayed electric vehicles that looked like Ferraris. My mom sat in one, ecstatic; upon learning that it cost the equivalent of $25,000 USD, she exclaimed that we should get one as our next car. For $55 USD, I got a pristine mani-pedi with beautifully applied builder gel and colour. For $3, I bought happiness in drink - tieguanyin milk tea with taro bubbles, perhaps the only boba David has given his stamp of approval on.

I spent my time in China equal parts marveling at its technological advances and wishing that there weren't such large barriers to entry into its ecosystem. I’d love to share China’s rich culture and city life with friends, because boy, was life in the fishbowl excellent.

## Jumping fish

Seeing that we took time off work, David and I often walked around Shenzhen’s large-scale malls on weekdays. Even during daytime hours, the mall was packed with lively customers. Everyone was eating, shopping, buying, and wandering around arm in arm with their friends. As we walked by a lotto stand, my cousin pointed out a number of what looked like early-twenty-somethings hunched over scratching cards.

“烧香拜佛,” she said.

“Huh?” I didn’t catch it.

“Praying to gods,” she repeated. “For a job.”

Youth unemployment rate in China climbed to 20% in June. That means one in five 16-to-24-year-olds who are looking for employment are not able to. The [census bureau has stopped releasing numbers as of August](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/08/15/business/china-youth-unemployment.html) because, as we all know, a problem unmeasured is a problem no more. The lotto stand is a curious byproduct of the dismal employment scene; in the face of overwhelmingly stacked odds, there’s a bit of comfort in leaving things to luck. That’s also why visits to Chinese temples are at an all-time high.

For those who have a job, they have two paths: one is to 卷 (juǎn, “grind”) in the 996 culture - wake up, work, sleep, rinse and repeat, 9AM to 9PM, six days a week. The other is to [躺平 (tǎng píng, “lie flat”), to do only the very bare minimum to get by in life](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rLDGBYJwFTo&list=LL&index=3&t=1s&ab_channel=SouthChinaMorningPost). That movement has evolved further to 摆烂 (bǎi làn, “to let rot”), a particularly evocative descriptor. Graduating students feel like they have no control over the situation; no matter how hard they work, they can’t sustain hope for a better future, so why not just let it rot?

Unemployment crises are far from a uniquely Chinese problem right now, but it’s exacerbated by the scale of the Chinese population and by other bubbles that are rising to the surface. As we were driving to a bustling beach town with polished high rises, my dad pointed out a set of apartments in a community to me. “That’s developed by 碧桂园 (Country Garden), one of China’s biggest real estate developers,” he said. “We were thinking about purchasing a unit, but pulled out because its financials were trending poorly.” At $3B USD, Country Garden was one of China’s biggest real estate developers. It’s also [facing default](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/08/15/business/country-garden-china-property.html?searchResultPosition=5).

If we had been visiting purely for fun, I would’ve never guessed this - not from the booming malls with futuristic mushroom architecture, not from the people mountain people sea wave of tourists at Hongya Cave in Chongqing, and not from the immaculately dressed Chinese fashionistas who dripped with more style and money on one wrist than I did from my entire body.

But the reality was that Chinese youth (and by youth, I also mean us aging millennials) were also struggling with a slowdown that’s crushing life goals like home ownership and income stability. The grass wasn’t always greener on the other side; US and Chinese youth are just on the opposite ends of the same pasture. But China had traded the US-style right-wing-left-wing finger pointing for public silence on debatable political policies and figures. A lot of the hopelessness and frustration simmered beneath the surface.

When I left China in 2012, user generated content moderation was nowhere as robust as it is today. Now, it’s like covering a frying pan with a splatter guard; no matter how hot the oil gets, it hits a wall before it can get out.

## Salmon always return home

The reason I went back this time was simple: my grandpa was 91. In the seven years I’d been away, he’d grown shorter, and he walked slowly, leaning on a cane on one side and a caretaker on the other. When I was a kid, I would lecture my grandpa: “no smoking, it’s bad for your health!” and he’d laugh, repeating “okay, okay,” while putting out his cigarette. This visit, I saw him reach for a cigarette and motioned to stop him, but my uncle intervened. “Let him,” he said. “At his age, let him enjoy it.”

In his aged voice, my grandpa quoted an idiom to me and asked whether I knew what it meant. When I shook my head no, he couldn’t hide his disappointment. “Jiayi-ah,” he said, “your Chinese knowledge is lacking.” It took all that I had to keep the sting of his words away from the smile I hung on my face.

What does it mean to be Chinese? At a most basic level, it’s my and my parent’s race and ethnicity. But beyond that, am I qualified to consider myself Chinese? If I’m not under the same beauty standards, political policies, employment conditions, social media sphere, news sources, cultural norms, education system, historical events, geographical influences, and community that an equivalent late-twenties Chinese individual is, if I return just to play physical and cultural tourist in my own home, am I really home?

I’ve often struggled with the common immigrant conundrum: required to westernize to fit in, but feeling less than whole if we throw our home culture away. Chinese diaspora get the extra benefit of being told we’re either CCP shills if we say anything positive about China, and country betrayers if we express any concern. But throughout my trip, I found that to see China clearly is to acknowledge both the benefits the fishbowl brings and the glass enclosure that makes it possible.

My parents threw a 出阁宴 (chū gé yàn, a Southern-Chinese traditional “bride-giving banquet”) for David and I this trip. They went the whole nine yards, renting out 秀禾 (xiù hé, a pseudo-traditional wedding outfit) for us to wear during the tea ceremony, decorating the banquet hall with Chinese ornaments, and inviting my maternal relatives across Guangdong to attend. David and I ping-ponged between frantically practicing our Chinese speeches and relishing in the joy of the moment. During this ceremony, and maybe throughout the entire trip, I found that I didn’t mind being neither fully Chinese nor fully western. It was enough for me that learning my language, reconnecting with my culture, and widening my perspective made me happy.

I have a vague memory of watching salmon return to spawn at Vancouver’s Capilano Salmon Hatchery when I was young. It was fun to watch the salmon struggle upstream and leap into the air, but I also thought it was funky and kind of stupid. Why travel all the way back to where they were born just to lay eggs and then die? Why spend all this effort fighting against the current? Sounds like a waste of energy. But salmon are programmed to return home; they can’t help the way they are.
